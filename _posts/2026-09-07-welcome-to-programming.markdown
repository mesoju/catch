Welcome to `Programming`! <br><br>

Coding the robot has been a serious endeavor of mine for the past 2 years as a programming executive, and it's definitely a rollercoaster of a journey. It's not an easy feat at all, and it has definitely taught me patience. Coding an FRC Robot is well, for a lack of better words, the most *frustrating* and *rage-inducing* task I have ever had to commit to. It's demotivating, and it's **painful**. The robot will not even have been made and physically birthed into the world yet before you are asked to create code for it in the hopes that when it is born that it will work. Most computer science classes (if you have ever taken any) have something called a **Run** button that you can press and it tells you every single error with your code at any moment you'd like. Coding for Robotics on the other hand, you don't have access to the magical debug button UNTIL 2 months after you have created all the code in theory. Chances are when you finally get the robot to push code to, you'll be met with a catastrophic error and have to redo your entire codebase or adapt.

`but here's the good news...`

I have already been through that ninth circle of despair, and likewise I have grown more knowledgable of coding with *WPILib* and an abundance of wisdom that I can use to make sure it goes smoothly this year, hence the existence of `CATCH.` I have the **utmost respect for you** because you've read through my honest take on the challenges of programming, and yet you're still here perhaps with the idea to code the robot alongside me, and eventually carrying on my legacy. Programming is **hard**, I will admit that any day of the week, but it also becomes the `most satisfying`. Your code **WILL** fail, but sometimes you have to realize that that is the thrill of robotics and problem-solving. I'm also researching simulation to make sure that even without the robot, you can still code.

You will put in all this effort over the course of a year, and at the end of it the robot that you've dedicated your time and effort to will come alive. While the other sub-teams are continuing to struggle optimizing and maintaining the robot after their initial glory of constructing the robot in the first place, you are the final piece of the puzzle, the ribbon of the gift, and you will turn effectively what was just a bunch of metal beams strung together into something that can score and win. You are the reason why our robot got us to Worlds. You are the reason why this robot will be used to teach the next year of robotics students and to set a standard that this club hasn't yet seen. 

`This was my philosophy for last year, the exact thing I'm telling you is what I told myself.` It sounds deep and edgy, I knooooowwww, but programming practically has a bell-curve. It's easy at first, but mind-numbingly boring, and then it gets stressful when you're writing code and figuring out what in the actual freak happened to the robot and why something just DOESN'T want to work like it should. And then once that motor that you've fought with moves in the way that you specifically told it too, you'll feel the adrenaline of the accomplishment. I yelled as loud as I could around 8 PM one day when the turret actually worked, and I hope that you'll find the same reaction that I once had.

## What Should you Expect in Programming?
My goal for this year is to really immerse all programmers what it was like for me and Phillip when we were the only two programmers working on this bot, as well as prepare each of you to potentially becoming a club executive. There will also be more activities and collaboration efforts this year because we have some new fun gadgets like GitHub for example. I truly want programming to be a sub-team that you will enjoy being in and also teaching to newer members eventually.
### For First Semester:
We will be learning the basics and fundamentals of FRC Programming and Java, and we will likely be applying what we learn to work on the REBUILT 2026 Season Robot, K2.
### For Second Semester:
By this time, Kickoff will begin and we will have to closely work with Mechanical and Fabrication to understand what their ideas for the robot are, as well as our own inputs into the matter. We should ask questions and also confirm whether some design choices are viable at all. Last year, we neglected the fact that our turret angle could not adequately aim high enough causing us to scrap the turret hood angle subsystem altogether as well as the code for it (and the code worked great too!). After ideas and designs are underway, we'll be given a rough schematic and determine how the robot will work, which is the fun but challenging part. We will develop code based on our understanding of the game and how the robot would move while in **theory** meaning that we won't have a single clue if our code works or not until we actually get the robot. Once we get the robot, it's dedicated entirely to us until the first competition where we have to test the robot on the field and be sure that everything works as intended and the robot is able to score points. During this time, the robot is also likely to break under our testing, which is **OKAY** and we should allow Mechanical / Electrical to maintain the robot as issues arise. I think the best philosophy to have is that ***good robots don't break with bad drivers***.

## Are there any Prerequisites to Join Programming?
No, just as any other sub-team, there are no expectations of prerequisite knowledge about coding to join. However, prior knowledge is encouraged as it makes learning how to program the robot much easier and less abstract; programming is very information dense. If you're coming to this sub-team fresh, I absolutely applaud you and I will take care of you when it comes to understanding how to code the robot within a semester, I will make sure of it, but you have to put in the same effort that I have put into you. :D

## Do I need any Materials to Join Programming?
Not at all, we have some laptops (albeit they're not the best and we're planning to get more) that you can use to help collaborate on code. We still **highly** encourage that if you have a laptop for school (not a Chromebook, but anything that supports MacOS or Windows) that you bring it on meeting days so that you can have a more hands-on experience when working with the robot.

## How much should I commit to Programming / Robotics as a whole?
Again, I won't expect anyone to be actively contributing to the club / sub-team beyond meeting hours on Wednesdays, just as any other sub-team wouldn't expect you to. However, since Programming is definitely a lot of information to absorb, I still highly encourage you to join, help, and ask questions beyond meeting hours to get to better understand coding. **CATCH** was made specifically for this purpose where in your free time, you can study the robot in a more efficient way as I handle the hard part of collecting and studying documentation, then delivering to you topics that are much easier to grasp. Programming can also act as a more online-oriented sub-team, and if you can't make it to Wednesday meetings, you can still watch **CATCH** so that you don't miss anything, and also if you happen to contribute on a week where you couldn't come to a Wednesday meeting, please reach out to me and I can hopefully work out your attendance. ^_^

I am here for anything that you need from me, and I have made a vow to be the reason for the success of future programmers to come to this club. I plan for **CATCH** to teach you exactly everything you need. Articles and videos for programming will be posted soon.

`hope to see you soon.`
